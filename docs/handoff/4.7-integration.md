## 2. Time logging: reused, not rebuilt

**Service jobs write `project_time_entries` rows.** There is one timesheet in
this product.

A technician's three hours on a customer's site is the same *fact* as a
consultant's three hours on a project: one person, one day, so many hours,
billable or not, at a rate copied onto the row, frozen once billed. Everything
that made `project_time_entries` the shape it is applies unchanged here —
including the locking rule in `ProjectTimeEntry::booted()`, which
`ServiceBilling` relies on rather than reimplementing.

Two columns changed, in `..._000704_log_service_time_on_the_existing_timesheet`:

- `service_job_id` — nullable, new;
- `project_id` — **made nullable**, because a service visit for a walk-in
  customer belongs to no project, and the alternative is inventing a hidden
  project to hang it on.

Both links can be set at once, and are: `ServiceScheduling::logTime()` reads
`project_id` off the ticket, so a service contract run as a project sees these
hours in its `costToDate()` and `unbilledHours()` with nobody logging anything
twice.

**Challenge this:** making `project_id` nullable weakens a constraint on a
table Projects owns. The alternative — a polymorphic `loggable` on the time
entry — is tidier in the abstract and would have meant rewriting every existing
query, factory and screen that assumes `project_id`. If Phase 5 adds a third
consumer of time, do the polymorphic version then, in one migration, with the
two known consumers to test against.

## 4. The SLA clock — every decision, and what to argue with

### 4.1 Deadlines are stored as absolute instants

`response_due_at` and `resolution_due_at` hold the answer, already walked
through the working calendar. The alternative is storing only the target and
computing the deadline on read.

Why stored: the breach board is the screen a service manager leaves open all
day, and it must be `where resolution_due_at < now()` on an index. Computing on
read means loading every open ticket and running calendar arithmetic per row —
fine at fifty tickets, hopeless at four thousand, and it also makes "show me
everything breaching in the next hour" impossible to express in SQL.

The cost is that deadlines can go stale if the policy's calendar is edited
afterwards. **Not handled.** A screen that edits a policy should recompute the
open tickets on it — `SlaClock::apply()` per ticket, ideally queued. That is
the largest known gap in this phase.

### 4.2 The deadline is recomputed, never nudged

    deadline = businessHours.add(opened_at, target + paused_minutes)

The obvious implementation is incremental: on resume, push both deadlines
forward by the pause. It is wrong, and it fails quietly. Escalate a ticket that
has been paused twice and you either lose the credited time or double it, and
nobody finds out until a customer disputes a breach months later. Recomputing
from opening is idempotent — run it ten times, in any order of pauses,
escalations and reopenings, and the answer is identical.

### 4.3 What stops the clock

Two situations, which are really one — *the ticket is not on the desk's plate*:

1. **`pending_customer`** — waiting on the customer.
2. **`resolved`** — sitting fixed, before somebody reopens it.

(2) is the non-obvious one. A ticket resolved on Monday and reopened the
following Monday must not arrive already breached; the business was not failing
to fix something it had been told was fixed. So `resolve()` stamps `paused_at`,
and `reopen()` credits the working time back exactly like a customer pause.

**Credit is measured in working minutes, not wall time.** A customer who takes
the weekend to reply has cost the desk nothing, so it gets nothing back;
crediting 65 hours for a two-day silence would make the resolution target
meaningless.

### 4.4 Business hours

`App\Support\BusinessHours` — a schedule of windows per weekday, a timezone, a
holiday list, and a `continuous` mode for 24/7 contracts.

- **The calendar lives on the policy, not in company settings.** It is a
  commercial term: the same business sells 24/7 to a hospital and 08:00–17:00
  to a corner shop, and both are true simultaneously.
- **`clock` is a named mode** (`business` / `calendar`) rather than "an empty
  schedule means always open". A policy whose windows were deleted by accident
  would otherwise silently become the strictest contract in the product; as
  built, it throws.
- **The weekday is read in the policy's timezone.** Asking "is it Saturday" of
  a UTC instant is wrong by up to a day for anyone off Greenwich.
- **A day is a list of windows**, so a lunch break is two windows rather than a
  special case, and a `22:00–02:00` night shift rolls into the next day.
- **Boundary convention:** minutes that exactly fill a window land on *closing
  time*, not the next morning's opening. Same instant in working time; 17:00 is
  the one a human reading "due 17:00" recognises. Worth confirming with whoever
  writes the SLA reports.
- **400-day walk guard.** A policy whose only open day is also on the holiday
  list would otherwise spin until the request timed out, and the bug would look
  like a hung server rather than a bad policy.

### 4.5 Breach semantics

- **Late is late.** Once `first_response_at > response_due_at`, it stays a
  breach — answering again does not clear it.
- **No policy, no deadline.** Both columns are null and `hasBreached()` is
  false. A board that invents promises nobody made will report breaches nobody
  agreed to, and the desk will rightly stop believing it.
- **A target may be null on one clock only.** Plenty of businesses promise a
  response time and nothing about resolution.
- **`atRisk()` measures its horizon in working time too**, per policy. A
  wall-clock horizon would sweep the whole Monday queue onto the warning board
  every Friday evening, and a warning that fires every week gets turned off.
  Cost: one `orWhere` group per policy. Fine at tens of policies; if a tenant
  ever has hundreds, bucket them by identical calendar.
- **`breach_notified_at`** stops `service:sla-sweep` shouting about the same
  ticket every run. Cleared on reopen.

### 4.6 Known limits

- Policies are per company, not per customer or contract. `sla_policy_id` is on
  the ticket, so resolving a customer's policy at open time is a one-line
  change when contracts exist.
- No "pause outside business hours for a calendar-clock policy" — by
  definition, a 24/7 promise has no such thing.
- No first-response-by-channel rules (email vs. phone).

## 5. What the owning phases need to add

### 5.1 Permissions — `app/Support/Permissions.php`

```php
'Service' => ['view', 'create', 'update', 'assign', 'schedule', 'complete', 'bill', 'manage-sla'],
```

Reasoning, in the register's own terms:

- **`view` / `create`** — a receptionist takes the call. Separate from
  `Customers.create`: logging a fault is not editing the customer file.
- **`update`** — editing the subject, category or description of a ticket.
- **`assign`** — deciding whose day this lands in. Separate because a
  dispatcher schedules other people's work without having any business
  rewriting the fault report.
- **`schedule`** — booking a visit. The dispatcher's ability.
- **`complete`** — signing a visit off as done. This is the one the technician
  gets, and it must be separate from `bill`: the person who says the work is
  finished should not be the person who decides what it is worth. Completing
  also closes an `AssetMaintenance` record and raises the next one, which is
  real consequence.
- **`bill`** — drafting the invoice. Sits with `sales.create`-level trust.
- **`manage-sla`** — editing a policy. Whoever can edit a policy can widen
  every promise the business has made until nothing ever breaches again, which
  makes the whole measure decorative. Should stop at the Owner and the
  Administrator in the seeder, like `workflows.manage`.

There is deliberately **no `service.approve`** — being asked by the workflow
engine is the permission, per `docs/workflows.md`.

Gates follow automatically from `AuthServiceProvider`'s catalogue loop. Until
these are seeded, every service ability is denied to everyone but the Owner —
so this is the blocking item for any UI.

### 5.2 `config/modules.php`

A `service` module, off by default, in the same tier as `projects`. It should
**not** gate `Contact` or `FixedAsset` — a business that turns service off must
keep its customers and its asset register.

### 5.3 Routes (`routes/web.php`)

```
/service                      SlaBoard  — breaching, at risk, unassigned
/service/tickets              queue, filterable by status/priority/assignee
/service/tickets/{ticket}     one ticket: events feed, jobs, SLA state
/service/jobs                 the diary — SlaBoard::diary()
/service/sla                  policies and targets (manage-sla)
```

Plus API under `/api/v1/service/*`, and `docs/API.md` §23.

### 5.4 Scheduler (`routes/console.php`)

```php
Schedule::command('service:sla-sweep')->everyFifteenMinutes();
```

The board is correct without it — deadlines are stored, so breaches are
computable on demand. The sweep exists only to *tell* somebody.

### 5.5 Listeners

`service.ticket.opened`, `.assigned`, `.reprioritised`, `.resolved`, `.closed`,
`.reopened`, `service.job.scheduled`, `.completed`, `.billed`,
`service.sla.breached` are emitted through `EmitsDomainEvents`, so
`AutomationRule` already sees them with no wiring. What is missing is a
notification on `service.sla.breached` to the assignee and their manager —
model it on the existing `workflow.stalled` notification rather than a new one.

## 6. Design decisions worth challenging

1. **`project_id` made nullable** (§2) — the polymorphic alternative and why it
   was deferred.
2. **The fourth invoice-line assembly** (§3) — extract `InvoiceDrafter`.
3. **Stale deadlines after a policy edit** (§4.1) — nothing recomputes them.
4. **Resolved counts as paused** (§4.3) — generous to the desk. A business that
   sees tickets being resolved-then-reopened as a dodge will want this off, and
   it is one line in `TicketDesk::resolve()`.
5. **Deadlines land on closing time** (§4.4) — check against how the business
   reports SLA attainment.
6. **Parts move no stock** — `service_job_parts` records what was fitted and
   prices it, but writes no `stock_movements`. Van stock is Inventory's ledger,
   and writing to it from here would give one physical count two owners. A
   business tracking van stock will need Inventory to issue against the job.
7. **`close()` back-fills `resolved_at`** if it was never set, so a ticket the
   customer considers finished does not show as never resolved.

## 7. The screens (phase 4.7 UI)

Three Livewire components, all over the services and none over the models:

| Component | View | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `App\Livewire\Service\Index` | `livewire/service/index` | The board first (breaching, at risk within four *working* hours, the six counters), then the filterable queue. Raise, assign and reprioritise inline. |
| `App\Livewire\Service\Show` | `livewire/service/show` | One ticket: fault, event feed, visits, time, parts, and the clock with what stopped it and why. |
| `App\Livewire\Service\Policies` | `livewire/service/policies` | Per-priority targets and the working calendar. `service.manage-sla` at mount *and* render. |

Notes for whoever reviews them:

- No countdown anywhere. Deadlines are printed as the instants the backend
  computed on the policy's calendar. A ticking "2h left" would be counting
  hours the business is shut, which is the exact lie §4 was built to avoid.
- `service.bill` is a separate button from completing a visit, and the screen
  shows only a *link* to the drafted invoice — no totals, no lines.
- The stale-deadline limit in §4.6 is stated on the policy screen itself,
  rather than left for somebody to discover.
- Every service call is wrapped and its `RuntimeException` message shown
  verbatim through `addError`, because those messages are already written in
  the business's language.

### 7.1 Routes — `routes/web.php`

```php
use App\Livewire\Service\Index as ServiceIndex;
use App\Livewire\Service\Policies as ServicePolicies;
use App\Livewire\Service\Show as ServiceShow;

// Gated on `service.view`, not on the stricter abilities: a receptionist needs
// to see the queue before being trusted to schedule anybody's day, and the
// buttons on the screen carry their own gates.
Route::get('/service', ServiceIndex::class)
    ->middleware('can:service.view')->name('service');
Route::get('/service/tickets/{ticket}', ServiceShow::class)
    ->middleware('can:service.view')->name('service.tickets.show');
Route::get('/service/sla', ServicePolicies::class)
    ->middleware('can:service.manage-sla')->name('service.sla');
```

`/service/tickets` (a bare queue) and `/service/jobs` (the diary) from §5.3 are
not built. The queue is the body of `/service`; the diary wants
`SlaBoard::diary()` and a date picker, and nobody has asked for it yet.

Until these are registered, the views link with `url('/service/…')` rather than
`route()`. Swap those three `url()` calls for `route()` when the routes land —
they are in `index.blade.php`, `show.blade.php` and `policies.blade.php`.

### 7.2 Navigation — `config/opes.php`

```php
['key' => 'service', 'label' => 'Service desk', 'icon' => 'wrench-screwdriver', 'route' => 'service', 'ability' => 'service.view'],
```

Ability-gated like the rest, so it disappears by itself for a business with the
module switched off — `service.view` is denied outright then.

### 7.3 What the screens assume

- The `service` module must be **on** for any `service.*` gate to pass. Tests
  set `modules => ['service' => true]` on the company; a business does it in
  Settings → Modules.
- Tests: `tests/Feature/Service/ServiceScreensTest.php`.
